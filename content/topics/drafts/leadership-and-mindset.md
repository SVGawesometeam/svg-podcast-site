## Marina's take

What does it actually take to build and lead a company, and keep going? I have interviewed founders for 10 years and run my own companies for 14.

It takes longer than you think. My rule from those conversations: meaningful results show up after about five years of all-in work. LinguaTrip took four years to reach a VC round; my creator business took seven. Amjad Masad spent 10 years on Replit and says the most underrated strategy is not quitting.

It takes a co-founder you trust. Raj Gokal calls the co-founder relationship the most important part of starting a company, and gave Anatoly Yakovenko six months to prove Solana before committing. Dmitri and I scaled LinguaTrip from 5 to 60 people in under six months, and some investors still called a married couple a red flag. We stopped working together in 2019.

It takes a filter for saying no. My revenue doubled in 8 months once I dropped everything except the interview podcast. Yamini Rangan asks five whys. Marie Forleo checks her nervous system: dread means no. Andrey Khusid stopped planning past 12 months.

And it costs something. Oscar Hoglund's wife told him their life was better when he was away. Roman Saini had panic attacks once a month during Unacademy's worst stretch, when the company was losing $150 million a year. Blake Scholl got down to seven days of cash. The guests do not agree on how hard to push. That is the next section.

## Where the guests disagree

How hard do you push, and for how long?

- **Blake Scholl** (Founder and CEO, Boom Supersonic): Budgeted for two failed startups before he quit Amazon at 33 with a toddler and newborn twins. Boom has a near-death moment about once a year, and he kept going through this one. "We got down to seven days of cash and the HR team and the legal team had a plan to file for bankruptcy." (/episode/NX5-bXM1NQg/, at 20:33)
- **Oscar Hoglund** (Co-founder and CEO, Epidemic Sound): His favourite word is relentless, and he has it on sweatshirts. "It's probably going to take 70, 80-hour weeks for many, many years before you even lift." (/episode/-Epp6VmpezI/, at 23:59) After his breaking point he rebuilt around one word, discipline, with three kids at home.
- **Marie Forleo** (Founder, MarieTV and B-School): Built her career on doing whatever it takes, reached burnout, and changed the rule. "it's like really honoring what my nervous system is a big yes to versus what is a big no." (/episode/IdN-b2Y_foo/, at 00:42) Dread about a product, a hire or an offer is a no, whatever the upside.

## Questions people ask

### Do I need a co-founder?

Most of the guests say yes, chosen carefully. Raj Gokal (/episode/tzUAJZAuaZw/) left his first startup partly over a misaligned partnership and gave Solana six months before going all in. Grant Lee (/episode/LAEaSbZ2IR8/) picked co-founders from his previous company with different skills from his own. Dmitri and I (/episode/KnWKFp472GI/) worked well together for nearly a decade and still chose to stop in 2019; the episode has our advice for couples and friends.

### How do successful people make big decisions?

With a repeatable question. Yamini Rangan (/episode/lVV0HInr0ZY/) drills five whys into any plateau. Oscar Hoglund (/episode/-Epp6VmpezI/) runs a three-why check before building anything with AI. Marie Forleo (/episode/IdN-b2Y_foo/) uses a full-body yes or no. Mine (/episode/APTVFZSYtiE/) is a list of things to say no to: busy work, false urgency, free business-class speaking trips that cost a week of output.

### How do I handle the hard days?

Nikil Viswanathan (/episode/kvlMhVz-DeM/) says Alchemy was the team's 16th pivot and that confidence and persistence are the two traits that matter. Jenny Lei (/episode/_tQUyvTLaSM/) sold no bags on launch day and later passed $9 million a year. Ankur Jain (/episode/PmpaFWBFy_c/) heard no from landlords and rewards partners for two years before COVID turned the calls around. Blake Scholl (/episode/NX5-bXM1NQg/) would rather be in the dark matter of founders who tried and failed than among those who never tried.

### What about burnout?

It happens to the people in these interviews too. I nearly quit YouTube in 2023 and needed a two-month break before the ideas came back (/episode/t6UbvaOGIEI/). Oscar Hoglund (/episode/-Epp6VmpezI/) and Roman Saini (/episode/mtfB8GtJAWM/) both describe their breaking points. Cassey Ho (/episode/4jqrK-hUicQ/) talks about the emotional load of leading a lean team. Marie Forleo (/episode/IdN-b2Y_foo/) now optimises for what she calls easeful money.

### Does hustle still work?

It depends who you ask, and when in life. Brian Chesky (/episode/iJlyNupMOzA/) says put blinders on, practise quietly for years, and earn attention later. Oscar Hoglund (/episode/-Epp6VmpezI/) expects 70 to 80-hour weeks for years before anything lifts. On my 35th birthday (/episode/QvwI0TTVcwo/) I told people in their 20s to work like crazy while they have the energy; eight months of saying no (/episode/APTVFZSYtiE/) is what doubled my revenue at 35. Both are true at different stages.
