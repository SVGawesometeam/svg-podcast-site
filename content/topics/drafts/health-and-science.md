## Marina's take

How is AI changing medicine, and how should I use it for my own health?

Dr. Jonathan Chen ran the study where GPT-4 on its own beat doctors who were using GPT-4. A third of the doctors had never used a chatbot. His reading is that AI wins at retrieving knowledge and amplifies however you ask: give it context and it sharpens a diagnosis, lead it and it confirms your mistake with confidence. Before an appointment he suggests putting your records and lab history into an AI and asking what questions to bring.

I have familial hypercholesterolemia, and Anne Wojcicki explained why no diet fixes it: a monogenic variant overrides the environment, while polygenic scores put you on a bell curve you can shift. 23andMe's data set is 14 million people, and risk prediction improved non-linearly past a million.

Dr. Priscilla Chan is building virtual cells, AI models of a human cell that let scientists run experiments on a computer. CZI has put over $7 billion into curing or preventing all disease since 2015. John Martinis won a Nobel for quantum tunneling and says quantum computers could crack Bitcoin encryption in 9 minutes, so the internet needs new encryption within five to ten years. The Liberman brothers expect biotech tipping points within two years. Richard Socher wants superintelligence pointed at physics, chemistry and biology.

This is what the guests said, not medical advice. Chen's own rule: use more than one model and ask for a second opinion.

## Where the guests disagree

Should a healthy person get full-body scans and extra tests?

- **Dr. Jonathan Chen** (Physician, computer scientist, Director of AI in Medical Education at Stanford Medicine): For someone asymptomatic, most early-detection tests are a dead end; the screens that work, breast and colon, were studied broadly, and attempts at pancreatic and ovarian screening failed. "They're not talking about the other thousands of people getting random scans and blood tests, checking for early cancer and really not finding anything." (/episode/oOpF0ZXSXuk/, at 24:05)
- **Anne Wojcicki** (Co-founder and CEO of 23andMe): Does a Prenuvo scan every year and annual low-dose chest CTs because her own risk rose after her sister Susan's cancer, and says the validated core is baseline genetics, blood tests and the preventive screens that fit your risk. "I recognize there's people who are anxious because you'll find all kinds of things." (/episode/VF2FwZ_PXhw/, at 30:00)

## Questions people ask

### Is AI better than my doctor?

At retrieving knowledge, sometimes. Dr. Jonathan Chen (/episode/oOpF0ZXSXuk/) found GPT-4 alone outperformed doctors using GPT-4 on diagnostic reasoning, partly because a third of the doctors had never used a chatbot. He says the doctor's value is context, trust, the final judgment call and knowing when more screening does harm. Dr. Priscilla Chan (/episode/nlOmBneT-oM/) left pediatrics for science and describes a new role for physicians working alongside AI.

### How do I ask AI about my health without getting a wrong answer?

Dr. Jonathan Chen (/episode/oOpF0ZXSXuk/) says people without medical training did worse with AI than without it because their questions steered it; describe symptoms without suggesting the diagnosis, give it your records, and check more than one model. Anne Wojcicki (/episode/VF2FwZ_PXhw/) coaches people on blood values, cancer genetics and lifestyle prompts, like asking for ten questions about your lifestyle given a factor such as APOE.

### Should I get genetic testing?

Anne Wojcicki (/episode/VF2FwZ_PXhw/) says every person can benefit, and names hereditary cancer, cardiovascular variants and communities with higher rates of conditions like BRCA, where knowing changes what you do. She separates monogenic variants, which override lifestyle, from polygenic scores, which you can shift by managing your environment. Dr. Jonathan Chen (/episode/oOpF0ZXSXuk/) keeps the family-history caveat: both his parents died of heart attacks in their forties, and that history is a reason to get checked, while healthy and curious is a different case.

### Will AI cure disease in my lifetime?

Dr. Priscilla Chan (/episode/nlOmBneT-oM/) thinks many diseases will be, and points to Baby KJ, whose mutation was corrected, and to treatments that did not exist when she trained at UCSF from 2012 to 2015. Anne Wojcicki (/episode/VF2FwZ_PXhw/) is the cautious one: clinical trials and the FDA still stand between a discovery and a drug, and the realistic five-year change is risk prediction. John Martinis (/episode/vZLY2YGUk4o/) sees quantum computers improving molecule simulation by 1 to 2%, which he says is worth significant money in drug discovery.
