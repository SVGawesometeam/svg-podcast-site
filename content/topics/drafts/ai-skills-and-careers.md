## Marina's take

What should I learn to stay employable as AI changes work? Three things come up in almost every interview in this hub.

First, use AI far more than you think you should. Conor Grennan, who trains companies through NYU Stern, counts a power user as someone who opens AI about 30 times a day. Kian Katanforoosh has tested over a million people at Workera and found that 71% misjudge their own AI level. Few use chain-of-thought prompts, few-shot examples or retrieval setups. That gap is where the pay sits: in the LinkedIn data from my seven-skills video, roles that require AI skills pay 28% more, about $18,000 a year. Reid Hoffman's deadline: serious AI habits before February 2027.

Second, learn judgment. Shishir Mehrotra has made promotion decisions for 20 years and now hires for what he calls eigenquestions, the ability to ask the right question. Yossi Matias hires at Google Research for the ability to think and adapt, ahead of any specific technical skill. I hire the same way. Taste and strategy first, because Claude already knows the facts.

Third, keep learning on a short cycle. Kian's data puts the half-life of a tech skill at about two years. Ryan Roslansky's LinkedIn data shows the skills a role requires change 25% every couple of years, heading for 70% by 2030.

The guests split on whether you still need to code. Thomas Dohmke says yes, and expects more developers in five years. Shishir says learn to manage instead.

## Where the guests disagree

Should you get more technical, or stop chasing tools and learn to run the work instead?

- **Thomas Dohmke** (CEO, GitHub): Coding skills still matter, and AI lowers the cost of learning them, so he expects the number of developers to grow. "I think we're going to have way more developers because it's so much easier to learn it." (/episode/PR__eFQsnhg/, at 04:48)
- **Shishir Mehrotra** (CEO of Superhuman, founder of Coda): Learning every AI tool is generic advice. The skill that pays now is management: deciding what good looks like and directing the execution, whether a person or an agent does it. "I think the more interesting advice is you learn to be a manager." (/episode/JEZVl3BkSLk/, at 02:56)
- **Yossi Matias** (Head of Google Research): When he hires, no single technical skill is the filter. He looks for people who can think, adapt and relearn as the tools change month to month. "the ability to adapt, to learn, to have a strong foundation is more important than ever" (/episode/PTZ5iN9nDkY/, at 06:50)

## Questions people ask

### Do I still need to learn to code?

The guests split on this. Thomas Dohmke (/episode/PR__eFQsnhg/) says a product anyone can prompt into existence in five minutes is not a business, and that the smartest companies will hire more developers, not fewer. Yossi Matias (/episode/PTZ5iN9nDkY/) hires for judgment and adaptability ahead of a specific technical skill. My reading: learn enough to direct and check the code an agent writes, then spend the rest of your time on judgment.

### How much AI use counts as real proficiency?

Kian Katanforoosh (/episode/Gk0n0FjeKj8/) draws the line between adoption and proficiency: daily chat is adoption, while chain-of-thought prompting, few-shot examples and retrieval setups are proficiency. Only 29% of the million people he tested judged their own level correctly. Conor Grennan (/episode/_aUKWYHLKfE/) measures it differently: about 30 uses a day, in long conversations rather than one-line searches.

### Which skill do the guests name most often?

Judgment. Shishir Mehrotra (/episode/JEZVl3BkSLk/) calls it eigenquestions and says it is now his top hiring signal. Max Tegmark (/episode/KF_uNAxPFFA/) points to McKinsey data across 70% of workplace skills showing employers pay most for critical thinking, decision-making and taste, and to an MIT study where 83% of ChatGPT users could not explain their own work minutes later. The skill that pays most is the one heavy AI use erodes, so practise it on purpose.

### Is there a deadline?

Reid Hoffman (/episode/S0h5oUVv0BY/) gives one: build serious AI habits before February 2027, or watch individual contributors get replaced by people who each run a suite of agents. Kian Katanforoosh (/episode/Gk0n0FjeKj8/) adds that the half-life of a skill in tech is about two years, so whatever you learn now needs refreshing by 2028.

### What should I do this month?

Reid Hoffman's answer (/episode/S0h5oUVv0BY/): before every task, ask how AI could help, even if you then decide not to use it. Kian Katanforoosh (/episode/Gk0n0FjeKj8/) gives a 90-day plan: foundations first on a platform like DeepLearning.AI, then plug into high-signal sources on X, Reddit and ML newsletters so you keep up as the tools change. Shishir Mehrotra (/episode/JEZVl3BkSLk/) adds one rule: practise new skills in low-stakes places, a side project or with friends, before your highest-stakes meeting.
