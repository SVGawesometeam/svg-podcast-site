## Marina's take

Is a degree still worth it? It depends on who you ask and what you want from it.

Samir Vasavada decided in seventh grade that he would not go to college. He founded Vise at 16 and reached a $1 billion valuation at 20. The one thing he thought a university offered was a network, so he built one with cold emails. Daron Acemoglu still says yes in ten years, though part of what you buy at Harvard or Stanford is the name. Sal Khan is building the alternative, a $10,000 degree with TED and ETS, and still hopes his son gets into a school where the family spends about half a million dollars.

On how to learn, the guests sit closer together. Max Tegmark walked me through the MIT study: ChatGPT users showed 55% less brain connectivity, and 83% could not explain their own work minutes later. Daron uses AI to criticise his drafts and warns against delegating the thinking that builds mental muscle. Esther Wojcicki says bans do not work; teach kids to use AI for feedback on their work, not to do the work. MacKenzie Price runs a school where kids do two hours of academics a day with AI tutors and score in the top 1% nationally.

In my own episode I pulled the numbers: entry-level postings down about 35% since January 2023, recent-graduate unemployment at 5.8%, and a 56% wage premium for workers with real AI skills.

## Where the guests disagree

Is a degree still worth the money?

- **Samir Vasavada** (Co-founder and CEO, Vise): Left high school at 16. The only value he could find in university was the network, and he built that with cold emails instead. - **Daron Acemoglu** (MIT economist, 2024 Nobel laureate): Thinks a university will still be worth the money in ten years, but a lot of what it sells is a signal, and the liberal arts path is not for everyone. ## Questions people ask

### Is a degree still worth it?

For the network and the signal, mostly. Daron Acemoglu (/episode/0lsasYuzhL4/) says yes in ten years, with the caveat that elite schools sell a name and not everyone belongs in a liberal arts program. Kian Katanforoosh (/episode/Gk0n0FjeKj8/) says people join top universities for the network and the ambitious people around them, and that the four-year bundle will change. Sal Khan (/episode/s-Iz-lLAhdg/) expects most students to go transactionally, and sees a $10,000 competency-based degree, possibly paired with a local community college, as a viable route.

### Does using AI make me worse at learning?

It can. Max Tegmark (/episode/KF_uNAxPFFA/) cites the MIT study where ChatGPT users showed 55% less brain connectivity and 83% could not explain their own work minutes later, which the researchers called cognitive debt. Daron Acemoglu (/episode/0lsasYuzhL4/) uses AI for background research and to criticise his drafts, but says the early evidence is that AI in middle and high schools without guardrails has made learning harder. Esther Wojcicki (/episode/qNt5TNn9D9Y/) draws the line at feedback: use it to check your work, not to do it.

### How should my kids learn with AI?

Esther Wojcicki (/episode/qNt5TNn9D9Y/) says bans fail, and points to prohibition in the 1920s; teach kids to use the tool critically and raise them on trust, respect, independence, collaboration and kindness. MacKenzie Price (/episode/WOmRB6jNiSU/) runs Alpha, where AI tutors deliver academics in 25-minute sessions, staff act as coaches, and students have moved from the 25th to the 90th percentile. Sal Khan (/episode/s-Iz-lLAhdg/) is rebuilding Khanmigo to be proactive, with the teacher kept in the loop.

### What should I study?

Daron Acemoglu (/episode/0lsasYuzhL4/) names math, physics, economics and engineering, plus the flexibility to change what you do within an occupation. Andrew Ng (/episode/o-wv_szZ0V0/) says universities teach for 2022 when they should prepare students for 2028, so supplement a degree with online courses. Yamini Rangan (/episode/lVV0HInr0ZY/) hires for three things: learning fast, handling ambiguity and thinking across functions. Aravind Srinivas, as I quote him in my education episode (/episode/eaZ2R1K-SjQ/), tells 18-year-olds to pick one direction and go deep for one to two years.

### Will AI replace teachers?

At Alpha, MacKenzie Price (/episode/WOmRB6jNiSU/) already uses AI tutors for content and hires coaches, athletes and business people for everything else. Gaurav Munjal and Roman Saini (/episode/mtfB8GtJAWM/) built Airarn to give instant feedback on English speaking and writing, which used to need human evaluators. Luis von Ahn (/episode/GDeEATJcbJo/) says demand to learn languages went up: Duolingo has more than 100 million active users, half of them learning as a hobby, the other half learning English.
