## Marina's take

Which jobs will AI change or replace, and how soon? The answer depends on whose data you read.

Ryan Roslansky reads LinkedIn's numbers. Entry-level hiring is down about 12%, but every level is down by the same amount, and he blames interest rates, not AI. He also counts almost 1.3 million new AI-related roles on the platform. Erik Brynjolfsson reads Stanford's payroll data. In the occupations most exposed to AI, employment for workers under 25 is down about 16%, and the effect grows every month. Saadia Zahidi at the World Economic Forum told me what insiders say: AI is real, and it is also a convenient excuse to fix the overhiring of the last boom.

On speed, the spread is wide. Yoshua Bengio measures the length of tasks AI can handle doubling every seven months and puts human-level systems about five years out. Daron Acemoglu, the 2024 Nobel laureate, would now put the share of fully automatable tasks a little above the 5% he forecast, and still thinks the debate exaggerates the pace, because integrating AI into organisations is slow.

What they agree on: tasks get reshuffled before whole jobs disappear, and the first rung of the ladder is the weakest. Erik's example is radiology, 26 tasks and AI touches one. My own pass through the BLS projections found 141 of the 206 most AI-exposed occupations still growing through 2035. If you are under 25 and in coding, support or admin, start with the episodes below.

## Where the guests disagree

Is AI already taking entry-level jobs, or is the slowdown something else?

- **Ryan Roslansky** (CEO of LinkedIn): Hiring is down at every level, and entry-level jobs are falling no faster than the rest, so he points at interest rates and company spending rather than AI. "the hiring rate we see is down roughly 12%. But they're not disproportionate to any of the other jobs." (/episode/ktpNFfpJ5rw/, at 03:33)
- **Erik Brynjolfsson** (Stanford economist, Director of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab): His lab ranked every occupation by AI exposure and found the effect concentrated in the most exposed ones and in the youngest workers, while home health aides and older workers are growing. "about 16% less employment for young people up to age 25" (/episode/72duHF7iZiU/, at 00:49)
- **Daron Acemoglu** (MIT economist, 2024 Nobel laureate): He has raised his estimate of what is automatable since 2024, but says the job-loss debate runs ahead of what organisations can actually absorb. "a lot of the discussion exaggerates how quickly we're going to see automation, because automation is hard." (/episode/0lsasYuzhL4/, at 00:00)

## Questions people ask

### Are the AI layoffs really about AI?

Partly. Saadia Zahidi (/episode/Dp6u0pel-Rs/) says the message from inside the room is that AI is real and also a perfect excuse to fix the overhiring of the last boom. Ryan Roslansky (/episode/ktpNFfpJ5rw/) sees hiring down at every level and blames interest rates. Luana Lopes Lara (/episode/xiJsfvaxzRY/) adds the market view: on Kalshi, 73% of trades in the monthly tech-layoff market name AI as the top reason for cuts, a flip from a year ago when users wanted markets on what AI could do.

### Which jobs are hit first?

Erik Brynjolfsson (/episode/72duHF7iZiU/) names coding, call centres and parts of sales and marketing. Jesse Zhang (/episode/UJo_MiMA7qE/), whose Decagon agents handle support for Hertz and Duolingo, puts tier-one customer support at the top of the risk list. Daron Acemoglu (/episode/0lsasYuzhL4/) disagrees on customer service: it is social and full of edge cases, and the automation he sees there is slow and bad. Coding went first because code either runs or it does not.

### Which jobs are growing?

Ryan Roslansky (/episode/ktpNFfpJ5rw/) counts 1.3 million new AI-related roles and over 600,000 data-centre jobs on LinkedIn. In my BLS breakdown (/episode/ael24TrS0ws/), healthcare adds about a third of all new American jobs, management analysts add 109,000 despite the highest AI exposure, and construction managers add 55,000 to build the data centres. Erik Brynjolfsson (/episode/72duHF7iZiU/) points to home health aides as the clearest growth at the low-exposure end.

### How soon does this happen?

The spread is five to ten years, depending on the guest. Yoshua Bengio (/episode/0fXGtQoJgNo/) sees AI task length doubling every seven months and human-level systems in about five years. Sal Khan (/episode/s-Iz-lLAhdg/) expects visible displacement in five to ten years, faster than he thought when he was an optimist. Daron Acemoglu (/episode/0lsasYuzhL4/) says the integration work is what slows it, and Kian Katanforoosh (/episode/Gk0n0FjeKj8/) notes that nearly every "this job disappears in months" prediction since ChatGPT has missed.

### I am early in my career. What do I do?

Erik Brynjolfsson (/episode/72duHF7iZiU/) tells junior engineers, marketers and paralegals to become the person who asks the right questions and evaluates the output, the two ends of the work that AI automated the middle of. Saadia Zahidi (/episode/Dp6u0pel-Rs/) gives a 30/60/90-day plan built on AI fluency, complex reasoning and group work, which she calls the most underrated career skill. Shishir Mehrotra (/episode/JEZVl3BkSLk/) says entry-level work does not vanish, it shifts upward, so learn to manage judgment earlier than you expected.
