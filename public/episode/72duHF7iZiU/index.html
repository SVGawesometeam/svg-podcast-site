About the Guest

Erik Brynjolfsson
Stanford Economist · Director of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab

Erik Brynjolfsson is a Stanford economist and Director of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab who has spent three decades measuring the impact of technology on employment and economic outcomes. He is the author of "The Second Machine Age" and recently published "Canaries in the Coal Mine," a widely-cited paper documenting AI's early effects on entry-level employment. He also runs Work Helix, a startup focused on helping companies maximize AI productivity, and teaches a master class at Stanford on leveraging AI for economic opportunity creation.

In this episode of the Silicon Valley Girl Podcast, Marina Mogilko interviews Erik Brynjolfsson, Stanford Economist · Director of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab. Erik Brynjolfsson, a Stanford economist who has spent 30 years measuring technology's impact on employment, reveals that AI has already eliminated 16% of entry-level jobs for workers under 25 in the most exposed occupations—a trend that accelerates monthly. However, the economic picture is more nuanced than simple job destruction: while coding, call centers, and parts of sales and marketing face significant displacement, other sectors like home health aide positions are growing, and some companies gain productivity advantages by using AI to augment rather than automate tasks. Brynjolfsson explains that the ultimate employment impact depends on demand curve elasticity—in roughly half the economy, increased efficiency from AI leads to lower prices and higher overall spending, which can actually create more jobs, not fewer. The key to thriving in the next 3-5 years is not coding itself but learning to manage fleets of AI agents and asking the right questions, skills that will remain valuable even as AI capabilities skyrocket.

Key Takeaways

  • AI has already wiped out 16% of entry-level employment for workers under 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations like coding and call centers, with effects growing larger every month according to ongoing research
  • No single job is entirely automated by AI—radiologists have 26 distinct tasks but only one (reading medical images) is significantly affected by AI, meaning hybrid human-AI work will persist across all occupations
  • Demand curve economics explain why AI efficiency doesn't always reduce hiring: when AI cuts prices in elastic markets (like air travel), total spending increases and companies hire more workers to meet higher demand
  • The next valuable skill is managing fleets of AI agents and asking the right questions rather than coding itself—at Stanford, all students now ship running code regardless of their background because AI tools like Cursor and Replit democratize development
  • The next decade could be either the best or worst in human history depending on whether we focus on the job-creation opportunities AI enables rather than just the automation that displaces workers
