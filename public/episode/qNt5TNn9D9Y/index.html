About the Guest

Esther Wojcicki
Educator, Author, and 'Godmother of Silicon Valley'

Esther Wojcicki is an educator and author known as the 'Godmother of Silicon Valley' who was one of the first teachers in California to use computers in the classroom starting in 1986, receiving computers directly from Steve Jobs. She is the mother of three accomplished daughters: Susan Wojcicki, former CEO of YouTube; Janet Wojcicki, a researcher at UCSF; and Anne Wojcicki, founder of 23andMe. Her parenting and educational philosophies have influenced countless families and leaders in the tech industry.

In this episode of the Silicon Valley Girl Podcast, Marina Mogilko interviews Esther Wojcicki, Educator, Author, and 'Godmother of Silicon Valley'. Marina Mogilko sits down with Esther Wojcicki, the legendary educator and mother of three accomplished daughters who shaped Silicon Valley. Esther discusses how she raised Susan Wojcicki (former YouTube CEO who convinced Google to acquire YouTube), Janet Wojcicki (UCSF researcher), and Anne Wojcicki (founder of 23andMe, once valued at billions). The conversation spans parenting philosophy, education in the AI era, and how to raise independent, creative thinkers. Esther shares her framework for success—the TRICK system (Trust, Respect, Independence, Collaboration, Kindness)—and explains why bans on technology don't work. She emphasizes that AI should be used as a collaborative tool to enhance critical thinking rather than replace it, and that the future workforce needs problem-solvers and creative thinkers who can work collaboratively.

Key Takeaways

  • The TRICK system (Trust, Respect, Independence, Collaboration, Kindness) is Esther's core parenting philosophy that has produced three daughters with vastly different but highly successful paths
  • Bans on technology don't work historically—alcohol prohibition in the 1920s and the war on drugs both failed; instead, teach kids to use technology intelligently and critically
  • AI should be used as a collaborative tool and smart teacher, not a replacement for human thinking—use it to get feedback on your work, not to do work for you
  • The skills employers actually want—problem-solving, creative thinking, and collaboration—require training that AI cannot replace; kids who let AI do their work are cheating themselves of skill development
  • Technology revolutions from computers in the 1980s to AI today require the same principle: learn how to use the tool effectively rather than fear or ban it
