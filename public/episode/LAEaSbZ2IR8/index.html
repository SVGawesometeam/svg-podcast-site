About the Guest

Grant Lee
Co-founder and CEO of Gamma

Grant Lee is the co-founder and CEO of Gamma, an AI presentation tool that has reached 100 million users and achieved a $2.1 billion valuation. He launched Gamma at the end of 2020 during the pandemic with two co-founders, growing the company to a team of 50 people. Prior to founding Gamma, Grant worked at Optimiz, where he met his co-founders before the company was acquired.

In this episode of the Silicon Valley Girl Podcast, Marina Mogilko interviews Grant Lee, Co-founder and CEO of Gamma. Grant Lee built Gamma, an AI tool that turns prompts into finished presentations, into a $2.1 billion company in just 4 years starting in late 2020 during the pandemic. He shares his journey of launching with no salary, a newborn daughter, and an investor who called it "the worst idea he'd ever heard." Despite these obstacles, Gamma grew to 100 million users with a lean team of 50 people. Grant breaks down the specific strategies that got Gamma to product-market fit, including how to run a fundraise in two weeks, how to identify if an idea is worth dedicating a year of your life to, and the two signals that indicate product-market fit before revenue proves it. He emphasizes that the biggest mistake AI founders make when fundraising is not thinking about distribution and organic virality from day one, and that friends will lie about your product—the real validation comes when people put their credit card down to pay.

Key Takeaways

  • Choose complementary co-founders with different skill sets over trying to build alone—Grant intentionally selected founders from his previous company who brought different capabilities to balance product, engineering, and business needs
  • Product-market fit shows up before revenue through two signals: customers willing to pay (credit card test) and organic virality built into the product mechanics from day one, not added later
  • Distribution must be architected into the product foundation for AI startups facing incumbent competition—build product-led growth and organic virality into the core philosophy rather than treating it as a later-stage problem
  • There is no perfect timing to start a company; founders should commit when they have an idea they think about night and day, full team alignment, and family support rather than waiting for ideal circumstances
  • Early negative feedback from investors can be a catalyst—Grant used the harsh criticism about facing massive incumbents as motivation to build distribution-first, turning a painful message into a core product strategy
